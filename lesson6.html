Section A

What is a Report?

Definition

A report is a comprehensive document that covers all aspects of the subject matter of study. It presents results of an experiment, investigation, research, or an inquiry to a specific audience.

💡 Used in business, academia, government, and scientific research

Section B

Characteristics of a Report

1

Presents information, not argument

Reports convey factual findings objectively — they do not persuade or debate.

2

Meant to be scanned quickly

Structure allows busy readers to locate key information fast.

3

Uses numbered headings & subheadings

Hierarchical structure helps readers navigate large documents with ease.

4

Short and concise paragraphs

Dense writing is avoided — clarity and brevity are prioritized.

5

Uses graphic illustrations

Tables, graphs, and pie charts support and visualize data findings.

6

May or may not have references

A bibliography is included when sources were used; otherwise it may be omitted.

7

Often contains recommendations and/or appendices

Practical suggestions and supplementary materials are typically included to support the report's conclusions.

Section C

Report Categories

Category 1

Informal Report

Functions to inform, analyse, and recommend. May be a memo, financial report, monthly activities report, or development report. Written according to an institution's style — introductory and prefatory parts are not required. Used for conveying routine messages.

Types of Informal Report

A

Progress Report

Provides information about how a project is developing.

B

Sales Activity Report

Helps a firm understand sales progress and identify shortcomings.

C

Personnel Evaluation

Used to assess an employee's performance.

D

Financial Report

Presents formal records of financial activities of a business or entity.

E

Feasibility Report

Assesses the viability of a new project and persuades decision makers.

F

Literature Review

Conveys existing knowledge and ideas established on a research topic.

G

Credit Report

Details an individual's credit history prepared by a credit bureau.

Category 2

Formal Report

An official report that contains a collection of detailed information, research, and data necessary for making decisions. It is formal, complex, and used at an official level — often a written account of a major project.

Examples include:

Launching a new technology or project line
Results of a study or experiment
A review of developments in a field
Formal & Complex Official Level Major Projects

Section D

Parts of a Report

Every formal report follows a standard structure. Each part serves a specific purpose and follows particular language conventions. Use this reference table to guide your report writing.

Part Description Key Contents Language Style
Title Page Name of the report and authorship information
  • Report name in ALL CAPS (e.g., FINAL REPORT)
  • Receiver's name, title, and organization
  • Team name and members
  • Date submitted & author's association
  • No page number on title page
 Factual
Table of Contents What you find in the report
  • Shows beginning page number for each heading
  • Connects headings to page numbers with dots
  • Headings must be grammatically parallel
  • Includes major sections and sub-headings
  • No page number on TOC page
 Factual
Executive Summary A summary of the entire report
  • No longer than one page
  • Provides key recommendations and conclusions
  • Not a summary of the document — highlights only
 Factual
Third person
Passive verbs
Introduction Background, problem, approach, definitions
  • Briefly describe the context
  • Identify the subject matter
  • Describe the issue or problem
  • State the specific questions the report answers
  • Outline the scope and preview structure
  • Comment on limitations and assumptions
 Factual
Third person
Passive verbs
Methods Procedures which led to the findings
  • Goal for each piece of research
  • Data source used
  • For surveys: number distributed, method, population sampling
  • For observations: how, when, and where it occurred
 Factual
Third person
Passive verbs
Findings Results, investigation & calculations
  • Supply proofs for conclusions
  • Discuss, analyse, and interpret data
  • Support findings with evidence
  • Explain all graphs in writing
  • Arrange in logical order matching methodology
  • Present "just the facts" — no opinions
 Factual
Third person
Passive verbs
Conclusion Conclusion drawn from the findings
  • Interpret and summarize the findings
  • Relate conclusions to the report problem
  • Limit to data presented — no new material
  • Number the conclusions in parallel form
  • Be objective; avoid exaggeration
 Transition signals
"it seems that…" "the results indicate…" "it is probable that…"
Recommendation Actions to be taken as a result
  • Make specific suggestions for actions
  • Avoid conditional words like maybe and perhaps
  • Begin each suggestion with a verb
  • Number the recommendations
  • Arrange from most to least important
 Action verbs
Bibliography References used in the report
  • Books, magazines, journals, reports
  • Online sources and other references used
  • Formatted according to the required citation style
 Citation format

Key Takeaway

Each part of a report has a distinct role. Following the standard structure ensures your report is professional, easy to navigate, and credible. Always use factual, third-person, passive language in the body sections.
