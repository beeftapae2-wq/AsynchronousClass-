What is Storytelling?

Storytelling is a powerful way to communicate emotion through improvisation or embellishment. It uses language, voice, movement, and gestures to reveal images and story elements to an audience. It is used by teachers, parents, and professionals to convey messages, entertain, educate, and preserve culture. (Alex, 1988 — stories play a significant role in students' growth and appreciation of literature.)

Why Use Storytelling in Teaching?

1

It Holds Attention and Motivates Learners

Cooter (1991) and Bla (1998) noted that excitement and drama provide a context that keeps students engaged. Stories motivate, create immense interest, attract listeners, and promote communication.

📌 Classroom Example: Instead of directly stating "bullying is bad," tell the story of a student named Marco who lost his best friend because of unkind words — watch how the class leans in and listens.
2

Stories Are a Treasure of Language and Culture

Storytelling is one of the oldest art forms. Thousands of moral stories have been created and passed down generations. Old stories are regarded as models of language and cultural treasures — learners at various age levels can find suitable stories to read and tell.

📌 Classroom Example: Sharing the Filipino tale "Si Malakas at si Maganda" not only teaches Philippine mythology but also naturally exposes students to rich descriptive language and cultural pride.
3

It Creates a Real-Life, Lively Atmosphere

Storytelling provides a lively atmosphere and real-life environment that encourages participation. When students listen to stories, they plunge into the plots and scenes and identify themselves among the characters.

📌 Classroom Example: Telling a story about a student facing a math problem in a marketplace helps learners connect abstract lessons to real situations — they see themselves in the story.
4

It Develops Listening and Comprehension Skills

Actively listening to stories strengthens students' ability to sequence events, infer meaning, and recall details — all critical literacy skills they apply across subjects.

📌 Classroom Example: After telling a short fable, ask students: "What happened first? What did the character feel? What would you have done differently?" This checks comprehension without a written test.
5

It Builds Empathy and Social-Emotional Learning

Stepping into a character's shoes through narrative helps students understand different perspectives, emotions, and life experiences — fostering compassion and emotional intelligence.

📌 Classroom Example: Sharing a story about a child from a different province or background helps students develop respect for diversity and reduces judgment.

Tips for Storytelling in Class

🎙️

Use Your Voice Dramatically

Vary your pitch, pace, and volume. A whisper can be more powerful than a shout.

👁️

Make Eye Contact

Move your gaze around the room so every student feels included in the story.

🎭

Bring Characters to Life

Give each character a distinct voice, posture, or gesture so students can tell them apart.

🤫

Use Strategic Pauses

Pause before a key moment to build suspense and hold attention.

🖼️

Paint Vivid Scenes

Describe sights, sounds, and smells so students can picture themselves inside the story.

💬

Involve the Audience

Ask questions mid-story: "What do you think will happen next?" It keeps everyone alert.
